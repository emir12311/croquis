# What Croquis does

The long version of the table in the [README](../README.md). Kept because the detail is
useful — how the breadboard conducts, what the current model does and does not, where a
saved file differs from Wokwi's.

## What works today

- **Desktop app in Rust**, drawn with egui — three dockable panels (sketch, parts, serial), resizable, each folding to
  its header with a chevron.
- **Code editor** in the Sketch panel: syntax colouring, completion from the Arduino API, auto-indent, and long lines
  **wrap**. No line numbers: the widget cannot wrap and number at once, and a line cut off at the panel edge hides code
  while a missing gutter only hides a count.
- **Two languages** — English and Turkish, chosen in **View → Language** and remembered between runs. The part and field
  names come from translation files, so a new part cannot ship an untranslated name.
- **Drag components** (Arduino Uno, LED and RGB LED, resistor, pushbutton, rotary and slide potentiometers, photoresistor, NTC temperature sensor, analog joystick, tilt/PIR/flame/gas/sound sensors, DHT22, HC-SR04, 4×4 keypad, IR receiver and remote, 74HC595, servo, stepper, LCD1602, buzzer, relay, 7-segment and 4-digit displays, LED bar, half or full breadboards) onto the canvas — the palette tiles show each part's **real drawing**, not a stand-in icon, and the canvas draws the same `wokwi-elements` art.
- **A breadboard that really conducts** — both a half board (30 columns, 400 tie points) and the **full board** (63 columns, 830 points). The five holes in a strip are one net, each power rail is one long net, and the two banks either side of the trench stay separate — so a wire into *any* hole joins the other four in its strip. Components **snap into the holes** when you drop or move them over the board, and a leg standing in a hole is **connected without a wire**, the way it really works. Dragging the board carries whatever is seated in it. The full board's power rails **break in the middle**, exactly like the real thing — the two halves are separate nets, so bridge them if you want power across the whole board.
- **Wiring, the Wokwi way:** click a pin and the wire follows your cursor; click another pin to connect; click empty space to drop a corner (route a wire around a part); **Esc** cancels. Clicking the **same two pins again removes the wire**. Wires are always **orthogonal** and draw **above** the parts, as they did in v0.2. Pressing near a pin and *dragging* moves the part instead — which is what lets you grab a breadboard anywhere, rather than only by its edges.
- **Reshape wires** — select a wire and **drag one of its corners** to move that bend, **drag an endpoint** to re-plug it into another pin, give it one of **eight colours**, or **Delete** it. Corners are the wire's own, not the waypoints the router happened to store, so moving a part that creates a new bend gives you a new corner to grab.
- **Rotate parts** (`R` steps 90°, `Shift+R` turns it 180°) — pins rotate with them.
- **Right-click a part or a wire** — an in-app menu (never a native one) with Copy / Cut / Duplicate / Rotate 90° / Rotate 180° / Delete, plus **Edit code** on the Uno, which raises the Sketch panel. Right-clicking a wire offers its colour and Delete. Right-clicking also selects what is under the pointer, the way v0.2 did.
- **Zoom, pan** — wheel zooms to the cursor; `Ctrl +` / `Ctrl -` / `Ctrl 0`; **right- or middle-drag pans**. Dragging with the left button on empty canvas draws a **selection box** instead, so panning never fights selecting.
- **Undo / redo** (`Ctrl+Z` / `Ctrl+Shift+Z`) over the circuit, plus **copy / cut / paste / duplicate** of parts and the wires between them. A drag is one step, not one per frame.
- **Menu bar** — **File / Edit / View / Run**, each item showing its shortcut. The primary actions deliberately *stay as buttons*: **Run lives in the circuit bar's top right**, and wiring, zoom and the palette never hide in a menu. The language lives in **View**.
- **Pin markers on hover** — a marker appears only for the pin under the pointer, on every part (not just breadboards: 400 hole markers read as static). A pin another part covers is not pickable, so a part lying over a board does not leak its pins through.
- **Pin tooltip** — hover a pin and a small label names it: *LED · A · Anode*, *Breadboard · a12 · strip 12 · upper*, *Pushbutton · 1.l*. It says what the element tells us, which is what makes multi-pin parts readable.
- **Editable part properties** — select a part and a **properties strip** under the circuit bar shows what you can change: an **LED's colour** (7 colours, swatched), a **resistor's value** — typed, in any SI unit from pΩ to TΩ, stored as ohms — the **potentiometer or slide-pot position**, a **photoresistor's light/threshold**, an **NTC temperature**, the **joystick axes**, **tilt/PIR toggles**, **flame/gas/noise levels**, the **DHT22 temperature/humidity**, the **HC-SR04 distance**, the **stepper's steps/rev**, and the **LED bar's colour**. It's **per part**, not per kind — two LEDs can be different colours — and it round-trips through `diagram.json` (Wokwi stores the same `attrs`), so a 10 kΩ resistor stays a 10 kΩ resistor. What's editable is declared by the catalogue, so a new component brings its own controls.
- **Presets** — **File → Presets** opens a nested menu, **Presets → group → circuit** (**Basics**, **Sensors**, **Outputs**) with a ready-made circuit (**Blink**, **Button**, **Potentiometer**, **Photoresistor**, **Thermometer**, **Slide potentiometer**, **RGB LED**, **Joystick**, **Digital sensors**, **Gas sensor**, **DHT22**, **Keypad**, **IR remote**, **Ultrasonic**, **Seven segment**, **Four digit**, **LED bar**, **Servo**, **Stepper**, **Shift register**, **LCD 1602**, **Buzzer & relay**, **Button + serial**, **Fade**) and its sketch, so you can press Run before writing anything. Each is a real `diagram.json` in the same shape Save writes, so a preset is opened by exactly the code that opens a file — and adding one is a new file in `presets/`, not a code change.
- **Where it differs from Wokwi.** Files are the same format in both directions, with two honest caveats. Wokwi's wire routes support a `"*"` separator that moves the remainder of the route to the *target* pin; we do not implement it, so a Wokwi file using it draws a different path here (the same pins, a different shape). And the extra fields this app adds — `sketch`, and a `title` on the presets — rely on Wokwi **tolerating** unknown fields rather than documenting that it does: real Wokwi files carry an undocumented `dependencies` field without trouble, so it is observed behaviour, not a contract.
- **Save / open** the circuit as Wokwi **`diagram.json`** — the same file Wokwi reads and writes, so circuits travel both ways. The sketch rides along in an extra `sketch` field. Saving is quantised to four decimal places, so a circuit does not creep every time it is opened and saved again.
- **New** starts a blank circuit without closing the app, and the app itself opens the same way — a blank canvas with the `setup()` / `loop()` template, not somebody else's example. **Save** writes straight to wherever the circuit lives (no dialog: the picker is for *choosing* a location, not confirming one), **Save as…** is how you move it, and a brief note confirms it landed.
- **Select several parts** by **dragging a box** on empty canvas, or **Ctrl+A** for everything. The selection can then be **moved, rotated, copied, cut, duplicated, pasted and deleted as a group** — dragging keeps the group's shape, and the right-click menu offers only the actions that apply to *every* part selected.
- **Compile & run** — press **Run**: the sketch is compiled with `avr-gcc` and executed on **simavr**; the Uno's built-in **LED (pin 13) blinks**, and a docked **serial monitor** prints what the sketch sends. Compiler diagnostics are listed with their file and line. While it runs the **circuit is frozen** (a strip says so) — the potentiometer's knob and its slider are the one thing that stays live — press **Run** again to apply an edited sketch, or **Stop** to change the wiring. The serial monitor also **sends** what you type to the sketch (`Serial.read()`), with an optional trailing newline.
- **Simulated parts** — wired components join the circuit: an **LED** lights when its pin goes high, and a **pushbutton** reads into the sketch (hold it to drive it — it *looks* pressed too). An LED's brightness follows the **PWM duty** of the pin driving it, so `analogWrite` fades it instead of flickering; an **RGB LED** has three independent channels and mixes their brightness. A **joystick** gives two ADC axes and a push-button on SEL. A **tilt switch** or **PIR** drives its OUT high while tilted/moving, and the module gets a green lamp; **flame**, **gas** and **sound** sensors expose an analogue reading and a comparator output that crosses at half scale. A **buzzer** plays a square tone at the driving pin's frequency (1 kHz for a steady high), a **relay** lights a coil badge and switches its P1/P2 contacts, a **1- or 4-digit 7-segment display** lights the segments whose pins are high (retaining each multiplexed digit between scans), and an **LED bar** maps each anode/cathode pair independently. **Servo**, **LCD1602**, **DHT22**, **4×4 keypad**, **stepper**, **IR receiver/remote**, **HC-SR04** and **74HC595** have their own bundled-library or protocol models: the servo pulses 1–2 ms every 20 ms, the LCD decodes 4-bit writes, the DHT22 answers with a one-wire frame, the keypad scans its matrix, the stepper advances its coils, the IR receiver decodes NEC frames, the ultrasonic sensor schedules its echo in simulated time, and the shift register latches eight outputs. A pushbutton shorts the pin to whichever rail it reaches: to **GND** it reads low while held, to **+5V** it reads high; released, the pin takes whatever else holds it — a pull-down resistor low, otherwise your pull-up high. A **resistor sets the current** it's in series with: 220 Ω gives a healthy ~14 mA, 1 kΩ still lights, 10 kΩ is a bare glow, and **an LED with no resistor is over-driven and reported rather than lit**, the way it would die on a bench. Colour matters too — a blue LED (3 V forward) draws less than a red one (1.8 V). It's a small series model, not a circuit solver: there are no arbitrary parallel networks, and the potentiometer is handled as a fixed divider rather than a general one.
- **Analogue input** — a **potentiometer** feeds `analogRead`: wire one end to **5V**, the other to **GND**, and the wiper (SIG) to an analog pin, and the sketch reads **value/1023 of the supply in millivolts** — 512 reads ~512 of 1023. Swap the end rails and the track reads the other way round, because that is what you built. **Turn the knob on the canvas while the sketch runs** — the same angle-drag the element itself has — or slide it in the properties strip; the circuit re-solves live, and each drag is one undo step. A potentiometer with an end left floating says so in the properties strip ("Put one end on 5V and the other on GND.") rather than quietly reading nothing; a wiper wired nowhere is left alone. The reference is the Uno's real one — the engine holds AVCC and AREF at 5 V, matching the board they simulate.
- **More analogue sources, one model** — the potentiometer's divider is the template the other analogue parts go through: a **slide potentiometer** (the same track on the element's 0–100 scale, read sideways, swapped ends read reversed like the knob), an **NTC temperature sensor** module (OUT rises across −15…135 °C — hotter reads higher) and a **photoresistor module** (AO sets with its Light slider; DO is its comparator, held high while light passes the Threshold slider). A module's rails are not a bare divider: wired backwards it does nothing and says "Its VCC pin needs 5V and its GND pin needs GND." rather than reading reversed. A powered module lights its **own indicator lamps** — power and comparator — wired or not, and the comparator **drives the pin it's wired to** both ways: below the threshold that pin reads low, not "whatever its pull-up says". All of them solve live from the properties strip and stay lazy about cache re-rasterising (`live` in the catalogue says which parts trim their sprites as they move).
- **Crisp rendering** — the canvas is drawn by egui at the display's own resolution. Parts are rasterised once from their Wokwi `<svg>` into textures, so zooming re-scales a sharp texture rather than re-parsing vector art every frame; the palette thumbnails are the same art at a smaller size.
